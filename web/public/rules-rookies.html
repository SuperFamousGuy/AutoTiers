Rookies & draft capital

Rookies are the hardest players to project, because they have no NFL usage history to anchor on. With no player-specific track record, the most predictive public signal available is draft capital — where the NFL itself chose to draft the player. A team that spends an early pick has told you, in the most expensive way possible, that it plans to give that player opportunity. These two rules turn that signal into a boost, calibrated to how reliably it holds at each position.

Both rules share the same logic. A team that invests premium capital in a player is committing to feed him — early-round backs inherit early-down and goal-line work, early-round receivers get run as route-runners and target earners — precisely to justify the pick. Draft capital is the market's aggregated pre-draft evaluation of talent and the best available proxy for the opportunity the player will actually receive. Crucially, both rules gate on the first two rounds only; day-three and undrafted players are treated as speculative and get no boost. A player whose draft round is unknown also gets nothing — the safe default, so an unmatched draft record never hands out a boost by accident.

Rookie RB Draft Capital +8%

Fires when a running back is in his first NFL season and was drafted in round 1 or 2.

Running back is the position where draft capital predicts rookie success most cleanly. Backs taken in the first two rounds convert to fantasy-relevant rookie seasons at a materially higher rate than later-round backs, who hit only sporadically. The reason is structural: early-round backs walk into defined, high-volume roles, and volume is what drives running-back scoring. AutoTiers adds 8% — the same magnitude as the Sophomore Leap — a deliberately modest number, because draft capital predicts a higher hit rate, not a guaranteed outcome, and the projections already carry some rookie hype that a larger boost would double-count.

Rookie WR Draft Capital +5%

Fires when a receiver is in his first NFL season and was drafted in round 1 or 2.

The same signal applies to receivers — but it is noisier, and the boost is deliberately smaller. Receiver outcomes depend on quarterback play, target competition, and a longer route-running development curve, so even premium capital converts to a productive rookie year less reliably than it does for backs, who inherit volume more directly. That is why this rule is set at +5% rather than the running back's +8%. The gap is intentional and load-bearing: setting the two equal would over-credit rookie receivers relative to what the evidence supports. Together with the Sophomore Leap, which covers year two, this rule fills the year-zero window for receivers.

A note on evidence: the direction of both rules — early draft capital means a higher rookie hit rate — is fantasy-analysis consensus, well supported across recent rookie-class studies. The exact round cutoff and the specific boost sizes are calibration choices weighed against the rest of the rule stack, not precise measured effects, and they are the sort of thing that gets revisited as more live seasons of feedback come in.
